# Turning PAN's walkie-talkie into a conversation

PAN can feel like a natural conversation on the phone and the CPU-only mini PC hub it already has, without waiting for a new kind of speech model: the walkie-talkie feel comes mostly from choices in today's code, not from missing hardware. Every stage after the user's last word runs one after another, and by the project's own measurements about two of the estimated 2.5 to 4 seconds before PAN speaks go to the fine-tuned 2B voice model (OmniΠ) re-reading its prompt and writing about 25 label tokens before the first word of its reply; PAN also stops transcribing while it talks, cannot be interrupted during the first 1.2 seconds of any reply, and several keyword shortcuts mute it, hijack ordinary sentences or throw away one-word answers. Sesame's Maya and the strongest 2026 systems show that a human feel comes from predictive turn-taking, instant yielding, a voice that follows the conversation, a consistent personality and a fast "talker" that covers for a slower "thinker", while today's open models that listen and speak at once score 4 to 58 percent on spoken reasoning and need 12 to 80 GB of GPU memory. The recommended path keeps PAN's chain of recogniser, language model and synthesiser (a *cascade*) but makes it streaming, speculative and able to listen while speaking: serving OmniΠ from llama.cpp's server with a cached prompt prefix, retraining it to speak before its labels, streaming audio into a streaming recogniser on the hub and starting speech at the first clause should bring first audio to roughly 0.65 to 1.1 seconds on CPU alone and 0.6 to 0.9 seconds when the shared RTX 3050 is free, against a human median of 0.1 to 0.2 seconds and a 0.7-second point where silence starts to sound like hesitation. Every one of those figures is an estimate assembled from component measurements on other machines, so the first deliverable is a two-channel test harness that plays recorded speech into the phone's real audio path and scores response gaps, false cut-offs, interruption speed, echo and memory. The largest commercial risks are that OmniΠ is built on Liquid's LFM3, which is free only below \$10M in annual revenue (rebasing it on Gemma 4 E2B, Apache-2.0 since April 2026, during phase 2 removes that ceiling), that the current Supertonic voice is no longer maintained upstream, and that the best-measured turn-taking components are locked to their vendors' frameworks or clouds.

*How to read the evidence.* Links into PAN's code point at this repository as of commit `35a621fd` (2 November 2026), so line numbers will drift. Figures marked *internal* are the project's own earlier measurements, collected with their provenance in the [current-state evidence map](../research_notes/PAN%20conversational%20voice%20plan/pan_current_state.md); none was re-measured for this report, and no end-to-end timing trace of the current build exists. Figures marked *estimate* are assembled from component benchmarks taken on other hardware.

## Human conversation sets a 700 ms bar, not a 200 ms one

Speech researchers measure turn-taking with the *floor transfer offset*: the time from one speaker stopping to the next one starting, negative when they overlap. Across ten languages the median offset after a yes/no question is **about +100 ms and the mean +208 ms** ([Stivers et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705608/)); in three corpora **70 to 82% of speaker changes happen within 500 ms and 82 to 95% within one second** ([Heldner & Edlund 2010](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/r.fernandezrovira/teaching/cosp/cosp2016/docs/HeldnerEdlund2010.pdf)); and across 1,656 recorded video calls the median is +80 ms ([CANDOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10065445/)). Those gaps are shorter than the time people need to plan speech, roughly 600 ms for a single word and 1.5 s for a sentence, so listeners predict where a turn will end, mainly from its grammar with intonation as support, and plan their reply while still listening ([Levinson & Torreira 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full)). Sesame's TurnBench corpus shows the result: in smooth handovers people start talking a **median 151 ms before** the other speaker has finished ([arXiv 2608.25218](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25218)).

The same data explain why "it cuts me off" and "it takes too long" arrive as a pair. **Pauses inside a turn are longer than gaps between turns**: in the Map Task corpora the median gap between speakers was 470-520 ms while the median pause within one speaker's turn was 580-640 ms, and a 500 ms silence threshold catches about half of the real turn endings but also 56-60% of mid-turn pauses ([Heldner & Edlund 2010](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/r.fernandezrovira/teaching/cosp/cosp2016/docs/HeldnerEdlund2010.pdf)). Any fixed silence timer short enough to feel quick therefore interrupts people who are thinking, and any timer long enough to be safe feels slow. Measured systems show the price of speed: LiveKit's turn detector cuts users off in **9.9% of turns with a 300 ms budget and 4.5% with a 600 ms budget** ([LiveKit](https://livekit.com/blog/solving-end-of-turn-detection)). What moves the median, for people and for machines, is prediction from words and intonation rather than a shorter timer.

Silence carries meaning well before the multi-second gaps people notice from machines. In telephone corpora, answers that arrive after **700 ms or more** are mostly dispreferred ones such as refusals ([Kendrick & Torreira 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/116177)); in experiments, gaps of 600 ms already lead listeners to expect an unwelcome answer ([Levinson & Torreira 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full)); in CANDOR, longer gaps go with lower enjoyment of the conversation ([CANDOR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10065445/)); and in a 2025 study of LLM-driven agents, delays beyond 4 s clearly degraded the experience, while natural conversational fillers helped and artificial "processing" indicators did not ([Maslych et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22352)). The realistic target is therefore not the human 200 ms but a typical gap below about 0.7 s and worst cases under about 1.5 s. Industry practice brackets that: practitioners aim for **about 800 ms voice to voice** ([Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/realtime-api); [AI Engineer](https://ai.engineer/talks/pipecat-cloud-enterprise-voice-agents-built-on-open-source)), Pipecat's own reference budget adds up to about 1.2-1.3 s ([Pipecat primer](https://voiceaiandvoiceagents.com/)), production agents across more than 4M calls run at a median of 1.4-1.7 s and a 90th percentile of 3.3-3.8 s ([SoftwareSeni, summarising Hamming](https://www.softwareseni.com/voice-agent-latency-solved-enough-for-production-benchmarks-and-architecture-tradeoffs)), and the fastest frontier speech products reach first audio in **0.44-0.62 s**, with "thinking" modes pushing the same models to 1.2-3 s ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-speech)). A 2026 survey puts the practical floor for a cascade at about 0.5 s once the turn decision fires ([full-duplex survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19453v1)).

Speed is only one axis. Benchmarks that play recorded users into live *full-duplex* systems, which can listen and talk at the same time like a phone call instead of taking strict turns like a walkie-talkie, find two opposite failure styles. OpenAI's GPT-4o Realtime stops within **0.23 s** when interrupted but answers **91-93%** of speech aimed at someone else or coming from a background voice, while Gemini Live and Amazon Nova Sonic ignore side talk but take about **2.2 s** to stop when genuinely interrupted ([Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23159)). A plain silence-triggered cascade takes the floor after 37% of user pauses where humans do so after 16% ([Talking Turns](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01174v1)), and a Whisper-to-GPT-4o-to-speech cascade starts talking before the user has finished in 33% of turns, against 13.5% for GPT-Realtime ([Full-Duplex-Bench v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04847v1)). Architecture is not destiny, though: in the ICASSP 2026 HumDial full-duplex challenge, a **cascaded entry ranked first** with a score of 76.6, ahead of Gemini 2.5 at 62.3 and every open end-to-end model (Moshi 34.5), while a plain cascade scored 37.7 ([HumDial study](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21406v1)); what separated the winner was its turn-decision module. PAN's scorecard therefore has to count cut-offs, how fast PAN yields, whether it tolerates *backchannels* (the "mm-hm" and "yeah" listeners use to show they are following), whether it ignores side talk and whether it remembers, not latency alone.

## PAN's serial, half-duplex loop wastes about two seconds a turn

PAN today is a strictly half-duplex cascade: separate parts recognise speech, write the reply and speak it, and only one side can talk at a time. On the phone, one 16 kHz microphone stream passes through Android's echo canceller and Silero voice activity detection (VAD), a tiny model that only answers "is someone speaking in this 32 ms slice?". While the VAD gate is open, a 70M-parameter streaming recogniser (a sherpa-onnx zipformer) decodes the speech; it produces running partial transcripts, but the service acts only on final ones ([SherpaStreamingStt.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/stt/SherpaStreamingStt.kt#L120); [PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L448)). After **0.35 s of trailing silence** the phone has a *candidate* end of turn and asks Pipecat's **Smart Turn v3.2**, an 8M-parameter audio model that listens to the last 8 s and returns the probability that the speaker has finished. It commits when that probability is at least 0.5 and the last word is not a "dangling" one such as "to" or "because"; otherwise it waits up to 1.4 s, and if any turn was committed in the previous 2.5 s ("flow mode") it demands 1.1 s of silence even after a finished clause ([SherpaStreamingStt.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/stt/SherpaStreamingStt.kt#L79); [FEATURES.md](../../../docs/FEATURES.md#L1162)). Some of the research notes behind this report describe a fixed 1.2 s silence rule; that is the sherpa-onnx library default, not what PAN runs.

Everything after the commit happens in sequence. The phone uploads the whole turn's audio as base64 inside one request ([PanServerClient.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/network/PanServerClient.kt#L300)); the hub re-transcribes it in one batch with NVIDIA's Parakeet TDT 0.6B, because the phone's audiobook-trained recogniser heard "what model is it using" as "what motto" ([FEATURES.md](../../../docs/FEATURES.md#L1192); [hub-asr.js](../../../service/src/hub-asr.js#L28)); and the router then builds every context block, awaits tool fetches of up to 1.5 s each and assembles a ~2,600-token prompt, all of which the OmniΠ path throws away ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L2155)). OmniΠ itself, a fine-tune of Liquid's LFM3-Omni-2B served by Ollama, is the largest block. *Prefill* is the model reading its prompt before it can write anything, and a *KV cache* stores the result of that reading so the next turn can skip whatever has not changed. LFM3 is a *hybrid* model that mixes convolution and short-window attention layers with a few full-attention layers, so its internal state cannot be rewound to an arbitrary earlier point; Ollama therefore re-reads OmniΠ's whole ~370-token prompt on every turn (**~1.3 s, internal**), the same failure llama.cpp users documented for the earlier LFM2 ([llama.cpp #16491](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/16491)). OmniΠ was also trained to write its labels in the order `to, addressed, intent, camera, response, …`, so about **25 label tokens (~0.7 s, internal)** come before the first word of the reply, although the router needs only `addressed` and `intent` before it may speak ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L1918)). Finally the phone waits for a full sentence, synthesises all of it, and synthesises the next sentence only after the previous one has finished playing, because synthesis runs inside the playback lock ([PiperTtsEngine.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/tts/PiperTtsEngine.kt#L319)).

| Stage | What happens today | Estimated time | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|

Five stages that could overlap therefore run back to back, and nothing at all uses the seconds while the user is still speaking. The repository's own feature notes claim OmniΠ produces its "first word in about 0.3 s on the mini PC CPU" ([FEATURES.md](../../../docs/FEATURES.md#L721)), which conflicts with the later, more specific internal measurement above; only a real trace can settle it. For scale, in September 2026, with a different model (gemma4:e2b) on the GPU desktop, PAN measured 1.6-2.0 s from question to speech on the phone (internal); today everything local runs on the hub CPU.

The half-duplex design also governs *barge-in*, the user interrupting while PAN talks. While PAN speaks, the recogniser is not fed at all; the first **1.2 s of every reply** only calibrates how loud PAN's own echo is, and after that the user must produce **700 ms of continuous speech louder than 2.2 times that echo** before PAN stops ([SherpaStreamingStt.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/stt/SherpaStreamingStt.kt#L642)). The earliest possible cut is therefore about 1.9 s into a reply, and a quick "no, wait" usually does not register. The stop itself is clean, flushing playback rather than draining it, but it does not cancel the in-flight request, so later chunks from the hub can restart PAN's speech ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L400)); any final transcript that arrives while PAN is speaking is discarded as probable echo unless it contains a stop word ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L1008)); and the history records the full intended reply even when the user heard only half of it.

Many of the "breaks in odd ways" are shortcuts misfiring before any model runs. The echo filter `stripEcho` returns an empty string whenever fewer than two words longer than one letter remain, whether or not any echo was found, so every one-word turn ("yes", "no", "why", "thanks") and phrases like "I agree" vanish ([SherpaStreamingStt.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/stt/SherpaStreamingStt.kt#L313)); that cancels the very improvement Smart Turn v3.2 was released for, **40% fewer misjudged one-word replies** ([Daily](https://www.daily.co/blog/smart-turn-v3-2-handling-noisy-environments-and-short-responses/)). Phone-side routing matches substrings, so "my commute was long" mutes PAN ("commute" contains "mute"), "I had to wake up early" is treated as an unmute command, "I have a meeting tomorrow" goes to the calendar and "check my reasoning" goes to a desktop Claude session with up to 45 s of polling ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L1020)). On the hub, an ambient-speech rule written for overheard lines that open with someone's name followed by "I'll meet you…" carries a case-insensitive flag, so "So I'm thinking we should redo the pendant" or "Honestly I'm exhausted" is silently classed as overheard ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L449)). A 0.35 s breath that Smart Turn judges complete commits a half-finished thought, and if the user resumes after PAN has started talking, the continuation is dropped as echo.

The "forgets what I said" complaint is mostly about which text reaches the model. OmniΠ's entire prompt is the phone's last ten history lines cut to 200 characters each, which include the current utterance a second time and PAN's own lines with latency suffixes such as "(1840ms)", plus memory hits when a recall pattern matches, a time line and the heard sentence ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L2402); [PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L175)). It contains no persona, no standing rules and none of the weather, news or encyclopedia data the router just fetched. The history stores the phone's garbled recogniser text rather than the hub's corrected text, keeps lines the hub classified as overheard, omits held replies that were later spoken, and differs in format from the training data (six lines, no duplicate, no suffixes). Questions such as "what were we just talking about?" are routed to long-term search instead of the live history, and when search finds nothing the hub answers "I don't have anything saved about that" in code, without ever showing the model the conversation it is being asked about ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L2048)). Standing instructions live in hub process memory and vanish on restart ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L406)).

Two structural limits sit in the model layer. The production gate that decides whether a line was meant for PAN reads OmniΠ's `addressed` score, which imitates the teacher model, while the 89-91% "for PAN" accuracy reported for OmniΠ was computed from the separate `to` field, which was trained on gold labels; with the earlier gemma model PAN still answered about half of overheard speech, and there is no wake word or speaker check on this path (internal). And the latest fine-tuning round concluded that the 2B student is at its reasoning ceiling, having been trained on only 376 distinct real voice sentences among 22,985 exported rows (internal). The point for planning is that almost every root cause above lives in orchestration and data, independent of whether PAN ever adopts a new speech architecture.

## Sesame's edge is presence, not a full-duplex model

Sesame's Maya and Miles are the usual reference for "talks like a person", and Sesame's own account of why is instructive. It names four ingredients: emotional intelligence, conversational dynamics (timing, pauses, interruptions, emphasis), contextual awareness and a consistent personality ([Sesame](https://www.sesame.com/research/crossing_the_uncanny_valley_of_voice)). Its Conversational Speech Model (CSM) generates compressed audio tokens with a large backbone and a small, fast audio decoder, and conditions every utterance on about two minutes of interleaved text and audio from both speakers, so intonation follows the conversation. The telling result: without context, listeners showed **no preference between CSM and real recordings**, so Sesame calls plain naturalness "saturated"; given 90 s of the preceding conversation, listeners **consistently preferred the human**, so the remaining gap is prosody that fits the moment ([Sesame](https://www.sesame.com/research/crossing_the_uncanny_valley_of_voice)). Sesame has never said which language model writes Maya's words; its open README recommends pairing CSM with a separate LLM, and one press report inferred a 27B Gemma ([SesameAILabs/csm](https://github.com/SesameAILabs/csm); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/sesame-releases-csm-1b-ai-voice-generator-as-open-source/)). Maya is, in other words, a cascade with an unusually good voice, not a monolithic listen-and-speak model.

Sesame's 2026 moves point the same way. Its October release gives each character "its own computer", so users can hand off trip planning or research hands-free and hear back when the work is done, and Sesame "reduced sycophancy and repetitive phrasing so characters can push back" ([TestingCatalog](https://www.testingcatalog.com/sesame-updates-voice-agents-with-their-own-computers/)). In August it published TurnBench, which scores end-of-turn and interruption detectors by recall at a fixed false-alarm budget on 30 h of annotated conversation, and found that **no system was fast, selective and high-recall at once** ([Sesame TurnBench](https://www.sesame.com/blog/turnbench)). The only open weights remain the base CSM-1B (Apache-2.0, March 2025): it needs a CUDA GPU and the gated Llama-3.2-1B tokenizer, is not tuned to any particular voice, and reached a *real-time factor* (compute seconds per second of audio; below 1 is faster than real time) of 0.383 on an L4 GPU, getting below 1 on CPU only after its decoding steps were cut ([SesameAILabs/csm](https://github.com/SesameAILabs/csm); [i-LAVA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20971v1)). What transfers to PAN is the recipe rather than the weights: a personality that pushes back and avoids stock phrases, which costs nothing but training data; prosody chosen from the conversation, which PAN can approximate now by letting OmniΠ choose Supertonic's inline expression tags and pace, and do properly later with a context-conditioned voice on a GPU ([Supertonic](https://github.com/supertone-inc/supertonic)); and slow work handed to background agents that report back, which PAN's existing relay to desktop Claude sessions already half-implements.

The frontier products have converged on three designs ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-speech)): native speech-to-speech models with selectable thinking levels such as OpenAI's gpt-realtime-2.x and Gemini Live; full-duplex voice front ends that delegate reasoning and tools to a backend agent, of which OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 is the clearest example ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-live-1)); and voice-vendor cascades tuned end to end, such as ElevenLabs' Eleven Realtime. Thinking costs time inside the same model: Gemini 3.1 Flash Live scores 71 on spoken reasoning with first audio at 0.96 s, and 97 at 2.99 s ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-speech)). Kyutai's open Unmute shows how a cascade earns a conversational feel with three tricks: an end-of-turn predictor inside the recogniser that weighs content and intonation, a "flush" that finalises the transcript about 125 ms after the user stops, and a voice that starts from the model's first words ([Kyutai STT](https://kyutai.org/stt); [Unmute](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/unmute)). Unmute itself needs a 16 GB GPU, but every trick ports to PAN's hardware. Deepgram's Flux adds the speculative piece, an "eager" end-of-turn event that starts the LLM early and a "turn resumed" event that cancels it; one integrator reports about 150 ms saved on average, at the cost of 50-70% more model calls ([Deepgram Flux](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/configuration); [Telnyx](https://developers.telnyx.com/docs/inference/ai-assistants/transcription-settings); [Deepgram quickstart](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/quickstart.mdx)).

The systems that get both naturalness and intelligence split the work between a fast *talker*, which owns timing and the first words, and a slower *thinker*, which reasons, retrieves and calls tools. KAME puts a Moshi speech front end ahead of a text LLM whose evolving answers stream in as hints: on a reasoning-heavy subset of MT-Bench (a standard multi-turn question set scored 1-10), Moshi alone scores 2.05, KAME with GPT-4.1 scores **6.43 at Moshi's latency**, and a conventional cascade with the same LLM scores 7.70 but takes 2.1 s to respond ([KAME](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02327v1)). ConvFill trains talkers of only 135M-1.7B parameters to absorb a large reasoner's streamed knowledge and lands **within 6.3% of the reasoner's accuracy** ([ConvFill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07397)). The no-training version is OpenAI's Chat-Supervisor demo, in which a realtime voice model handles greetings and small talk itself and calls a text model through a tool for anything harder, at a cost of about 2 s between its filler line and the answer ([openai-realtime-agents](https://github.com/openai/openai-realtime-agents)); Hume's EVI 3 handoff, NVIDIA's "on-hold" lines during tool calls and Kyutai's MoshiRAG, which hides retrieval inside the natural delay before a sentence's key words, are variations on the theme ([Hume](https://hume.ai/blog/introducing-evi-3); [AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/nvidia-opens-nemotronlabs-voicechat-11b-with-tool-calling); [MoshiRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12928)).

The open models that listen and speak at the same time are where naturalness is strongest and intelligence weakest. On Artificial Analysis' leaderboard they score well on *conversational dynamics* (pause handling, backchannels, turn-taking and interruptions) but poorly on *spoken reasoning* (reasoning questions asked by voice), where frontier products score 92-99%, and none runs in real time on a CPU:

PAN tried this route once: loading the 7B Moshi-based PersonaPlex with CPU offload exhausted the laptop's RAM in March 2026 and the effort was abandoned (internal), a hardware failure rather than a judgement on conversation quality. The evidence says not to retry it on current hardware: nothing good fits in 8 GB, and the reasoning gap would make even a working model a worse assistant than PAN's own model plus a thinker. The place for a full-duplex model is later, as a talker in front of PAN's thinker on a 16-24 GB GPU, the shape of KAME, MoshiRAG and GPT-Live-1. The one CPU-targeted end-to-end candidate is LFM3-Audio-2B, whose causal audio encoder can digest speech while the user is still talking; it is half-duplex, its voice chat is English-only, its CPU latency is unmeasured and it inherits LFM's revenue threshold, so it belongs in an experiment track rather than the main plan ([LFM3-Audio-2B](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-Audio-2B); [Liquid licence](https://docs.liquid.ai/lfm/help/model-license)).

## Six phases bring PAN to about one second on the CPU hub

The plan keeps PAN's cascade and changes its plumbing in six phases, ordered so that each is measurable before the next begins and so the cheapest, highest-leverage fixes land first. Phases 0 to 3 need no new hardware; the GPU and the cloud enter in phase 4 as optional accelerators, with the CPU path as the always-available fallback. Latency figures are estimates of the median time from the end of the user's speech to PAN's first audible sound, built from the component numbers cited in each phase; exit criteria refer to the scorecard defined in phase 0.

| Phase | What changes | First audio after the user stops, median (estimate) | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| Today | serial pipeline, prompt re-read, labels first, batch recognition, whole-sentence voice | ~2.5-4 s; interruption possible only ~1.9 s into a reply | nothing measured end to end |
| 0. Measure | two-channel harness, phone event log, hub timings saved to disk | unchanged, now measured | baseline for M1-M15 with known run-to-run spread |
| 1. Remove breaks | routing shortcuts, echo filter, barge-in cancel, history text, recall routing, unused tool work | ~0.2 s faster; up to ~1.5 s on tool turns | M3 ≤3%; zero hijacks on a fixed sentence list; M11 above baseline |
| 2. Stream and overlap | llama-server prefix reuse, answer-first retrain, streaming hub recognition, preemptive start, first-clause voice | ~0.65-1.1 s | M1 ≤1.0 s median and ≤1.8 s p90; M4 ≤10%; M2 not worse |
| 3. Listen while speaking | echo self-test, recogniser running during replies, layered barge-in, heard-only history, route-triggered preambles | unchanged for easy turns | M5 ≤0.5 s median; M6 ≥90%; M8 ≥80%; M10 ≤1 per 10 min |
| 4. Remember and escalate | server-side session log, append-only prompt, state block, session-level retrain, talker/thinker routing, GPU or cloud thinker | easy turns as phase 2; ~0.6-0.9 s when the GPU serves the turn; hard turns open with a preamble on that schedule | M11 and M12 ≥80%; M13 ≥70%; M14 ≥60% |
| 5. Presence and pendant | expressive voice on GPU, Supertonic successor, pendant streaming and echo cancellation, addressee prior | +~0.05-0.1 s when a GPU voice is used; pendant link unmeasured | M15 ≥50% win-or-tie; M9 ≥80% on the pendant |

The finish line, "feels like a person", is the scorecard's human-like column held for two consecutive weeks together with the owner's blind preference. On CPU alone its 0.6 s median is probably out of reach: the turn decision uses 0.25-0.35 s, a 2B model on DDR4 memory needs 0.35-0.5 s for its first clause and the phone voice 0.15-0.35 s more (estimate), so meeting it takes the GPU, a native-audio model or more aggressive end-of-turn settings. The CPU target is the gate column, which already sits inside the commercial range.

### Phase 0: build the two-channel harness before changing anything

None of PAN's existing tests can see the problem. The `TEST_SAY` debug intent injects text straight into the reply path, skipping the microphone, VAD, end-of-turn logic, hub recognition, echo cancellation and barge-in ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L2288)); the `converse.js` suite posts text over HTTP ([converse.js](../../../service/src/evals/converse.js)); the phone's `Stream (first=…ms)` log starts at query launch, so it excludes both end-of-turn time and synthesis; and the hub's timing lines go to a console that is not saved. The project's history shows what untested audio changes cost: switching the capture source to `VOICE_RECOGNITION` removed the echo canceller's reference signal and PAN "cut itself off on every reply after the first" ([SherpaStreamingStt.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/stt/SherpaStreamingStt.kt#L479)), and a spoken "thinking" filler that could not be cancelled made a 1.9 s answer reach the ear at nearly 4 s ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L1453)).

The harness follows the public full-duplex benchmarks and Pipecat's audio-mode evaluations: play real user audio into the real pipeline, record both sides on one clock, and score timing from audio rather than from logs ([Full-Duplex-Bench](https://github.com/DanielLin94144/Full-Duplex-Bench); [Pipecat Evals](https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat/evals/overview)). It needs a debug-only `TEST_AUDIO` intent that streams a WAV file, paced in real time, into the same consumer `AudioRecord` feeds, so VAD, the recogniser, Smart Turn, routing, the hub and the voice all run as in real use; a JSON-lines event log on the phone's monotonic clock covering injected frames, VAD transitions, partial and final transcripts, Smart Turn scores and inference times, commit and hold decisions, the request, the hub's `heard` event, the first reply chunk, the first text handed to the voice, synthesis start and end, the first audio written and the playback head; a tap of the audio actually played; hub timing lines persisted to a file; an orchestrator over adb that fires barge-ins and follow-ups at times relative to PAN's speech onset or offset, as EchoChain does, and uses an isolated conversation id so tests never touch real memory ([EchoChain](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16456)); and a scorer that runs Silero VAD on both channels, computes the scorecard, and reserves a local LLM judge for the few content judgements deterministic checks cannot make, since a CPU judge costs about 50 s per call (internal).

It runs in two modes. *Digital* mode, nightly, injects audio directly and is fully repeatable. *Acoustic* mode, weekly, plays the same timeline from a small speaker beside the phone so the real microphone, echo canceller and speaker-to-microphone echo are in the loop; it is the only way to measure echo self-triggers. A fixed script read once by the owner gives realistic disfluencies, accent and microphone distance (keep those recordings out of this public repository), synthetic variants add volume, and side talk, TV and noise are mixed with the published recipes ([Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23159); [tau-Voice](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13686v1)). Five scenario families come first because they map onto the complaints: quick plain turns; utterances with deliberate mid-thought pauses of 0.6-2.0 s; barge-ins with a new question 1, 2.5 and 5 s into PAN's answer; "mm-hm" and "yeah" during long answers; and facts planted 3, 6 and 10 turns before they are asked about. One-word answers, side talk and TV, mid-answer corrections, standing instructions and an acoustic echo stress test follow. Latencies are reported as 50th and 90th percentiles with bootstrap confidence intervals, never means, because gap distributions are skewed ([Heldner & Edlund 2010](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/r.fernandezrovira/teaching/cosp/cosp2016/docs/HeldnerEdlund2010.pdf)); a rate metric needs about 100 events per run to tell 10% from 5%, and a percentile about 40 transitions. Once a month, slices of TurnBench's development set and the English subsets of Full-Duplex-Bench v1/v1.5 and HumDial-FDBench go through the same harness so PAN can be compared with published systems; TurnBench's data is licensed non-commercially and forbids voice cloning, so it stays an internal check after its terms are read ([TurnBench](https://github.com/SesameAILabs/turnbench); [HumDial-FDBench](https://github.com/ASLP-lab/HumDial-FDBench)). A ten-minute weekly owner check closes the loop: eight blind A/B pairs built from the same injected audio, since pairwise judgements are more reliable than ratings ([AudioJudge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12705)), three minutes of free conversation with a hardware "that felt wrong" gesture logged against the event stream, and four 1-5 ratings (timing felt natural, it let me finish, it stopped when I wanted, it remembered).

The scorecard below is the contract every later phase is measured against: gate values must not regress between releases, and human-like values define the finish line. The thresholds are proposals anchored on the human data in the first section and on the best published systems, where GPT-4o Realtime stops in 0.18-0.23 s, Nova Sonic keeps talking through 98% of user backchannels ([Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23159)), the best trained end-of-turn models fire in about 5% of mid-turn pauses ([Sesame TurnBench](https://www.sesame.com/blog/turnbench)) and no real-time model passes 50% on EchoChain's interruption-state test ([EchoChain](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16456)).

| ID | Metric | How it is computed | Gate | Human-like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | Response gap, median / p90 | PAN audio onset minus user speech offset, complete turns only | ≤1.0 s / ≤1.8 s | ≤0.6 s / ≤1.0 s |
| M2 | False cut-off rate | PAN commits or speaks before the true end of a turn that contains designed pauses | ≤15% | ≤5% |
| M3 | Missed turns | complete user turns with no PAN speech within 5 s | ≤3% | ≤1% |
| M4 | Over-waits | complete turns with a gap above 2.0 s | ≤10% | ≤3% |
| M5 | Barge-in stop latency, median / p90 | PAN audio offset minus user onset on true interruptions | ≤0.5 s / ≤1.0 s | ≤0.25 s / ≤0.5 s |
| M6 | Yield success | true interruptions where PAN stops within 2 s | ≥90% | ≥98% |
| M7 | Response after interruption | addresses the new content; latency from user offset | ≥70%, ≤1.5 s | ≥90%, ≤1.0 s |
| M8 | Backchannel tolerance | "mm-hm"/"yeah" during PAN speech with no stop or restart | ≥80% | ≥95% |

### Phase 1: remove the self-inflicted breaks

The first code phase fixes behaviour no new model can fix, mostly a few lines at a time. On the phone, control words should match as whole words or whole utterances, so "commute" no longer mutes and "wake up early" is not an unmute, and calendar, email and "check my" requests should flow from the model's own `intent` output instead of substring matches ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L1020)). `stripEcho` should blank a turn only when echo words were actually found, so "yes", "no" and "why" survive ([SherpaStreamingStt.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/stt/SherpaStreamingStt.kt#L278)). A barge-in should cancel the in-flight request and the hub stream so PAN cannot resume by itself ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L400)). History should take the hub's corrected `heard` text instead of the phone's, drop the latency suffix and the duplicated current line so serving matches training, drop lines the hub classed as overheard, add held replies that were spoken, and keep only the sentences actually played before a cut ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L1480)). The sentence splitter should stop breaking at decimals and abbreviations, and the code defaults should name the sherpa recogniser and the Supertonic voice, because today's defaults (Google's recogniser and Android's system voice) would silently switch off Smart Turn after a reset ([PanForegroundService.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/service/PanForegroundService.kt#L123); [TtsManager.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/tts/TtsManager.kt#L41)). On the hub, the case-insensitive ambient rule should be deleted or repaired ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L449)); "what did I just say" and "what were we talking about" should be answered from the live history rather than long-term search, and the coded "I don't have anything saved" refusal should never fire while the session history is non-empty ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L2048)); standing instructions should move from process memory to the database; and context and tool blocks that the OmniΠ path discards should no longer be awaited before the model starts ([router.js](../../../service/src/router.js#L2197)).

Latency moves only slightly, by about 0.2 s on ordinary turns and up to 1.5 s on turns where a tool pattern fires (estimate), but the complaint list shrinks. The phase exits when missed short turns (M3) fall under 3% in the one-word scenario, a fixed regression list of everyday sentences ("my commute was long", "I have a meeting tomorrow and I'm nervous", "check my reasoning", "So I'm thinking we should…") produces zero hijacks or mutes, context recall one to three turns back (M11) is measurably above the phase 0 baseline, and the harness has measured how often OmniΠ's `addressed` gate holds or mutes lines that were meant for PAN. Whether to gate on the gold-trained `to` field instead should be decided on those numbers.

### Phase 2: stream and overlap every stage to reach about one second

Phase 2 attacks the two seconds of model time and the serial chain around it. The levers, in order of estimated size:

| Lever | Change | Estimated saving | Main risk to measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prefix reuse | serve OmniΠ from llama.cpp's `llama-server` (an LFM3-compatible build) and restore a saved state of the constant prompt each turn, or keep the conversation append-only so the previous turn's checkpoint stays valid | ~1.0-1.1 s | whether saving and restoring round-trips LFM3's convolution and sliding-window state on the build in use |
| Answer-first output | retrain the output order to one or two control tokens (addressed bucket, route), then `response`, then the remaining labels | ~0.6-0.8 s | gate accuracy must not drop |
| Streaming hub recognition | stream 20 ms audio frames during the turn and run Nemotron Speech Streaming 0.6B (English) on 2-4 hub threads; keep batch Parakeet for other languages | ~0.25-0.6 s | finalisation speed on Zen 3 with DDR4; CPU contention with the model |
| Preemptive start | at the 0.35 s candidate, restore the prefix, prefill the new text and start lookups; discard on a hold | hides ~0.25-0.35 s | wasted CPU on false candidates |
| First-clause speech | hand the first clause of 5-8 words to the voice and synthesise sentence N+1 while N plays | ~0.1-0.4 s, plus no dead air between sentences | phone synthesis speed never measured |

Prefix reuse is the largest lever and the least certain. llama.cpp's server has supported saved "context checkpoints" for hybrid and recurrent models since October 2025, along with explicit slot save and restore to file and a host-memory prompt cache ([PR #16382](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/16382); [server README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/master/tools/server/README.md)). A checkpoint only helps if it sits exactly at the end of the unchanged part of the prompt, and by default checkpoints are spaced at least 8,192 tokens apart, so PAN must either restore an explicitly saved prefix state each turn, prime a checkpoint at the prefix boundary with a one-token request (the workaround documented for LFM2), or keep the whole conversation strictly append-only so the end of the previous turn is always a valid restart point ([llama.cpp #16491](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/16491)). LFM3's state for a ~370-token prefix is only a few megabytes (about 4.4 MB of attention cache, 2.6 MB of sliding-window cache and 0.3 MB of convolution state, computed from the published architecture), so a restore should take milliseconds, and the 40-60 new tokens per turn then cost 0.15-0.2 s at the hub's measured ~285 tokens/s prefill (internal) ([LFM3 deep dive](https://local-ai-zone.github.io/blog/lfm3-deep-dive.html)). Two facts must be checked first: that the build correctly saves LFM3's convolution and sliding-window state, and that a llama.cpp build new enough for LFM3 GGUF files is available, since one cited build is not ([LFM3 deep dive](https://local-ai-zone.github.io/blog/lfm3-deep-dive.html)). If either fails, a full-attention small model needs no checkpoints at all.

Answer-first output needs a retrain but no new data: reorder every training target so one or two control tokens (an addressed bucket and a route such as camera, tool or standing instruction, in the spirit of Pipecat's single-token turn tags) come first, then `response`, then everything else ([Pipecat primer](https://voiceaiandvoiceagents.com/)). Speech can then begin after about one control token plus 6-8 reply tokens, roughly 0.25-0.35 s at the hub's 22-35 tokens/s (estimate), and labels needed only after the reply, such as importance or speech act, cost nothing audible.

Streaming recognition moves the hub's transcription inside the user's turn. Instead of uploading the clip after the commit, the phone streams 20 ms frames while the VAD gate is open: a WebSocket is adequate on a direct home connection, while WebRTC with Opus (a compressed audio format) over UDP avoids TCP's stalls on lossy cellular links ([LiveKit](https://livekit.com/blog/why-webrtc-beats-websockets-for-voice-ai-agents); [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/real-time-speech-to-text-under-200ms)). On the hub, NVIDIA's Nemotron Speech Streaming 0.6B (English, punctuated, commercial use permitted) was measured on a six-core Ryzen 7500F finalising in **58 ms at the median with six threads, 71 ms with four and 114 ms with two**, running about 15 times faster than real time ([Softcery](https://softcery.com/lab/hosting-nemotron-asr-streaming-on-a-cpu); [model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/nemotron-speech-streaming-en-0.6b)); on the older Zen 3 5800H with DDR4, about 70-150 ms is a reasonable estimate, against 0.37-0.77 s for today's batch pass. PAN already found this model about seven times too heavy for the phone ([FEATURES.md](../../../docs/FEATURES.md#L1201)), so it belongs on the hub, where its stable partial transcripts can also drive speculative prefill. Greek and other languages keep batch Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3, which covers 25 European languages but scores a 20.7% word error rate on Greek ([Parakeet v3](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3)). Before any of this is tuned, `tailscale ping` should confirm that the phone reaches the hub directly, because Tailscale's relay servers (DERP) are the slowest path and its own troubleshooting examples show relayed round trips of 441-478 ms ([Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/kb/1257/connection-troubleshooting/)).

Speculation and the voice close the remaining gaps. *Preemptive* (or "eager") generation starts composing the reply on a likely end of turn and throws the work away if the user keeps going; LiveKit enables it by default ([LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/tuning.md)). For PAN, the 0.35 s candidate is the trigger: restore the prefix, prefill the new text and start memory and tool lookups while Smart Turn deliberates, and discard on a hold. On an eight-core CPU a discarded guess competes with real work, so one speculation at a time is the limit and playback never starts before the commit. On the phone, the first clause (five to eight words, or up to a comma) should go to the voice instead of the first sentence, and synthesis should move out of the playback lock so sentence N+1 is ready when N ends ([PiperTtsEngine.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/tts/PiperTtsEngine.kt#L326)); at Supertonic's laptop real-time factor of 0.23 (internal), a 1.5 s clause takes about 0.35 s to synthesise against 0.7 s for a 3 s sentence. Background jobs such as embeddings and vision should pause during a live turn, with two to four threads pinned for recognition and the rest for the model, since the hub has a history of one job blocking another (internal). Finally, flow mode's 1.1 s wait keys on any commit in the last 2.5 s; once replies arrive within a second, quick back-and-forth exchanges will start tripping it, so it should key on whether PAN has spoken since the last commit, and the 0.35 s candidate itself should be tuned between 0.2 and 0.35 s against the false cut-off rate.

Together these give an estimated **0.85-1.45 s** from end of speech to first audio, or about **0.65-1.1 s** when recognition finalisation and the model's restore and prefill overlap the turn decision (estimate assembled from the component figures above). The phase exits when the response gap (M1) is at or under 1.0 s at the median and 1.8 s at the 90th percentile, over-waits beyond 2 s (M4) are under 10% and false cut-offs (M2) are no worse than after phase 1.

### Phase 3: let PAN listen while it speaks

Listening while speaking depends on *acoustic echo cancellation* (AEC): subtracting PAN's own voice, as played by the loudspeaker, from what the microphone hears. Android only requires that a device with AEC on its voice-communication path expose it, not that it work well, and field reports describe broken vendor implementations ([AOSP](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/implement-pre-processing); [Fairphone forum](https://forum.fairphone.com/t/echo-in-voip-calls/55836/18)). The phase therefore starts with a startup self-test that plays a known clip and measures how much echo remains on each audio route, a figure called ERLE (echo return loss enhancement). If the platform canceller passes, PAN keeps it; if not, the fallback is the raw recognition capture source plus WebRTC's AEC3 canceller fed the exact audio PAN is playing and a chirp-measured delay, available as an MIT-licensed Android extraction ([webrtc-aec3-kmp](https://github.com/Enaium/webrtc-aec3-kmp/wiki)), introduced only behind the acoustic harness given what the last capture-source switch did. Two cancellers should never be stacked, loudspeaker volume should be capped, and a device that fails falls back to tap- or keyword-only interruption.

With echo under control, the recogniser keeps running during PAN's speech and barge-in becomes a layered decision rather than a 0.7 s loudness test. Production stacks treat speech during playback as a candidate: Vapi stops after 0.2 s of speech by default, and LiveKit's learned interruption model needs a median of **216 ms** of overlapping audio to decide, with 86% precision and 100% recall, but it runs only in LiveKit's cloud ([Vapi](https://docs.vapi.ai/customization/speech-configuration); [LiveKit](https://livekit.com/blog/adaptive-interruption-handling)). PAN's open equivalent lowers its own volume after 200-250 ms of speech on the echo-cancelled signal; within the next 300-500 ms it yields if the partial transcript holds two or more words that are not backchannels or any stop word ("stop", "wait", "no", "hold on"), restores volume and carries on if it hears only "yeah", "mm-hm" or "right", and resumes from the last sentence boundary if no words appear within 1.5-2 s. The 1.2 s blind calibration gives way to a threshold that tracks the echo reference continuously, and at least a second of pre-roll keeps the start of an interruption, which today's 600 ms pre-roll, shorter than its 700 ms trigger, loses.

On a yield, PAN stops the voice, cancels the request and the hub stream, and stores its turn as only what was played, marked as interrupted, by mapping the playback position to a character offset; Gemini Live, OpenAI's Realtime API and Pipecat all keep only what the user actually heard ([Gemini Live](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities); [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/api-reference/realtime-client-events/conversation/item/truncate); [Pipecat](https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat/fundamentals/interruptions)). Overlap is not always a request to stop: in unscripted conversation people adapt their turn to a collaborative overlap **68.2% of the time, against 34.8% for PersonaPlex** ([Continue, Adapt, or Yield](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13117)). A cascade can approximate this by classifying the overlapping words on the hub as a backchannel, a correction or completion, a new request or side talk, and then continuing, adapting (a brief "right, Thursday", then regenerating the rest of the answer), yielding or ignoring. The same machinery repairs false cut-offs from the other side: if the user resumes within about a second of PAN starting, PAN yields and merges the continuation with the previous fragment as one turn, which in turn lets the 1.1 s flow-mode wait shrink.

Phase 3 is also where speech covers unavoidable waits. When the route token says a turn needs a tool, the camera or the thinker, PAN plays a short preamble that names the action, such as "Checking the forecast", drawn from a pre-synthesised and varied pool, cancellable at any moment and skipped if the answer arrives first. OpenAI's voice guidance prescribes exactly such one-line preambles before slow tools and warns against empty fillers like "Hmm…" ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-prompting)), and user studies find natural fillers help at long delays while artificial "processing" signals do not ([Maslych et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22352)). It differs from the filler PAN removed in September in being triggered by the route rather than a timer, and in being cancellable. During long user turns a silent "I'm listening" cue on the screen, and later the pendant's light or haptics, gives reassurance without any echo risk; spoken backchannels from PAN should stay rare and quiet if used at all. The phase exits when barge-in stop latency (M5) is at or under 0.5 s at the median and 1.0 s at the 90th percentile, with 0.25 s as the target, yield success (M6) is at least 90%, PAN talks through at least 80% of backchannels (M8), and acoustic runs show at most one echo self-trigger per ten minutes of PAN speech (M10).

### Phase 4: remember the conversation and hand hard turns to a thinker

Every production stack keeps far more history than PAN's ten lines. Pipecat's optional summariser does not start until 8,000 tokens or 20 new messages ([Pipecat](https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat/fundamentals/context-summarization)), OpenAI's gpt-realtime keeps up to 28,672 input tokens and can drop the oldest turns in large chunks so its prompt cache survives ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/blog/realtime-api)), and Gemini Live sessions have a 128k-token window that, with compression on, slides down when 80% full while always preserving the system instructions ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/live-api/sessions)). Phase 4 implements the project's July 2026 decision that conversation state lives on the server (internal), which the code follows only as a fallback when the phone sends no history: the hub keeps one canonical session log written from its own transcripts and rebuilds the prompt deterministically and append-only on every turn. That is also the layout that keeps phase 2's cache valid:

```
[static head]      persona, standing rules, output format, today's date     cached; changes daily
[append-only log]  user turns (hub text), PAN turns as heard,
                   tool and thinker results, instruction events             cached up to the last turn
[ephemeral tail]   exact time, memory hits, state block (≤150 tokens),
                   current utterance                                        re-read every turn
```

A text transcript is small, so a whole session should fit verbatim in LFM3-Omni-2B's 32K-token window ([LFM3-Omni-2B](https://huggingface.co/liquidai/lfm3-omni-2b)); real session sizes should be measured to confirm it. The log must render byte-identically from turn to turn, including LFM3's empty `<think></think>` markers, because any rewritten prefix forces a full re-read on a hybrid model. The state block, rebuilt off the critical path by the thinker or a background pass, lists active instructions with the turn they were set, language and style, the current topic, open questions, corrections with the latest winning, and facts established this session. Restating key state near the end of the prompt matters because even frontier models lose **39% on average** moving from single-turn to multi-turn tasks, and restating earlier information each turn recovers part of that loss ([LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06120v1)).

OmniΠ then needs a second, larger retrain on whole sessions of 10-40 turns rendered in exactly this layout, seeded with what PAN fails at: an instruction given early and tested ten turns later, "no wait, I meant…" corrections, references back to earlier turns, questions whose answer is not in context (where the right move is "I'm not sure, want me to check?" or escalation), recognition errors in user turns, replies cut to spoken length, and Sesame-style pushback in place of easy agreement. *On-policy distillation*, in which the student generates its own replies and the larger teacher scores every token, reached the quality of extended fine-tuning at 9-30 times lower cost and restored instruction-following that a narrow fine-tune had wrecked (IF-eval 85% falling to 45%, then back to 83%) ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/)); rewarding abstention over confident guessing follows OpenAI's analysis of why models hallucinate ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)). Clock questions should be answered from the system clock, not the model.

Intelligence beyond the 2B ceiling comes from the talker/thinker split, Chat-Supervisor style first. OmniΠ answers an allow-list of turn types itself (greetings, acknowledgements, confirmations, small talk, reading back results) and escalates fact lookups, live data, multi-step reasoning, references outside its context, its own uncertainty and standing instructions it handles badly, such as sustained translation. The escalation decision is OmniΠ's first output token, so the preamble starts on the phase 2 schedule and the thinker's answer follows in the same voice, written into the log as PAN's own turn so later turns remember it. A short preamble takes on the order of a second to say, comparable to a cloud model's median time to first token plus transport, so it covers most cloud turns and nearly all local GPU turns (estimate). Late results can follow Gemini Live's delivery rules: speak when idle by default, interrupt only for corrections that matter, or add silently to context ([Gemini Live tools](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/tools)). A KAME- or ConvFill-style talker that blends streamed thinker hints into an answer already in progress is the trained successor once session data exists.

The thinker runs on the shared RTX 3050 when it is free. Published llama.cpp numbers for Gemma 4 E2B on an 8 GB RTX 3050 show **3,860 tokens/s prefill, 116 tokens/s decode and 213 ms to first token** for a 512-token prompt in 2.77 GB of memory ([DEV](https://dev.to/gde/gemma-4-on-a-used-8-gb-rtx-3050-116-tokenss-for-one-user-618-for-64-k6h)); at that speed even OmniΠ would read its prompt in about 0.1 s, so sending every turn to the GPU while it is available gives an estimated **0.6-0.9 s** first audio (worst case about 1.2 s), with the CPU path taking over when the GPU is busy. The project previously saw multi-second prefill for ~1,900 tokens on that card and no gain from larger models on a 41-case corpus (internal), while the published rate implies about 0.5 s, so the GPU configuration should be checked before the card is written off. The thinker model should be chosen by PAN's own evaluation rather than vendor tables, which show LFM3 strong on instruction-following but well behind 4B-9B models on knowledge, as Liquid itself acknowledges ([Liquid](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/lfm3)). The cloud is the quality fallback, not the speed path: with local recognition and voice, a cloud model lands at an estimated 1.1-1.6 s median with a 95th percentile beyond 2 s, given cloud time-to-first-token medians of 536-739 ms and 95th percentiles of 1.1-1.8 s ([Pipecat primer](https://voiceaiandvoiceagents.com/)); PAN has recorded 46-56 s spikes on its Gemini fallback (internal), so every cloud call needs a hard timeout behind a local preamble. Speech-to-speech APIs, at \$0.20 a minute or more and with raw audio leaving the house, do not fit PAN's local, no-per-minute-cost goal ([Pipecat primer](https://voiceaiandvoiceagents.com/)). The phase exits when context recall (M11) and instruction persistence (M12) reach 80% at 3, 6 and 10 turns, corrections (M13) 70% and interruption state updates (M14) 60%, with no regression in easy-turn latency.

### Phase 5: give the voice presence and move to the pendant

With timing and memory fixed, the remaining Sesame gap is prosody that fits the conversation. On the phone, OmniΠ can choose Supertonic's expression tags and pace from context while PAN keeps one consistent voice. On the GPU, context-conditioned and expressive voices should be tested against the harness: CSM-1B with previous turns passed as context segments, Chatterbox Turbo (MIT, with an audio watermark) and Kyutai's TTS 1.6B (CC-BY-4.0, English and French, about 5.3 GB of GPU memory in Unmute's figures) ([CSM](https://github.com/SesameAILabs/csm); [Chatterbox](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox); [Kyutai TTS](https://huggingface.co/kyutai/tts-1.6b-en_fr); [Unmute](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/unmute)). A GPU voice adds an audio return trip of roughly 50-100 ms (estimate), and no first-audio measurements exist for these models on an RTX 3050. Supertonic also needs a successor, because its repository was archived on 9 September 2026 ([Supertonic](https://github.com/supertone-inc/supertonic)); CPU candidates include Kitten TTS v0.8 (Apache-2.0, English only) and Kyutai's Pocket TTS, whose licence must be re-read, while Greek text, being non-Latin script, already goes to Android's system voice ([Kitten](https://huggingface.co/KittenML/kitten-tts-mini-0.8); [Pocket TTS](https://huggingface.co/kyutai/pocket-tts); [TtsManager.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/tts/TtsManager.kt#L131)).

The pendant changes the audio path. The ESP32-S3 has Bluetooth Low Energy only, with no classic Bluetooth audio and no LE Audio ([Espressif](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-adf/en/latest/solution-center/bluetooth-audio.html)), and today PAN's pendant link carries only camera frames ([PendantBle.kt](../../../android/app/src/main/java/dev/pan/app/ble/PendantBle.kt#L38)). Audio should travel as Opus in 20 ms frames over Wi-Fi or a custom BLE stream to the phone, rather than the 60 ms frames of Espressif's reference voice component, which add about 40 ms of framing ([xiaozhi](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-iot-solution/en/latest/ai/xiaozhi.html)). If the pendant plays audio, echo should be cancelled on the pendant itself with Espressif's ESP-SR front end in its one-microphone-plus-reference mode, which uses about 19% of one core and 0.74 MB of external RAM (PSRAM) in its speech-recognition setting, under a licence limited to Espressif chips ([ESP-SR benchmark](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-sr/en/latest/esp32s3/benchmark/README.html); [licence](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp-sr/versions/2.6.0/license)).

An always-on wearable also makes *addressee detection*, deciding whether speech is meant for PAN, central, because fast-yielding systems answer side talk almost every time. PAN needs a prior for "the wearer is talking to me": the wearer is close to the microphone and louder than a TV, an enrolled-voice check can confirm it is the owner, and timing matters most, so wearer speech inside a conversation window after PAN's last reply is treated as addressed while speech outside it needs a wake word or high confidence. A 2026 vendor paper reports an audio-only F1 (a combined accuracy score from 0 to 1) of 0.86 at 38 ms on an ARM CPU and found temporal context the strongest cue, though its results are internal and unaudited ([SAS](https://huggingface.co/papers/2604.08412.md)). The long-term step, once a 16-24 GB GPU or an affordable cloud talker exists, is a full-duplex talker of the Moshi, PersonaPlex or MiniCPM-o 4.5 class in front of PAN's own thinker and tools, evaluated with the same harness; native audio input to LFM3-Audio, which Liquid says reaches first output 50 ms after speech ends, is the experiment to rerun whenever llama.cpp audio support and measured CPU speed allow ([LFM3-Audio-2B](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-Audio-2B)). The phase exits on the owner's blind preference (M15) of at least 50% win-or-tie against phase 4 and side-talk rejection (M9) of at least 80% with the pendant.

## Licences and unmeasured numbers are the real risks

### One revenue threshold sits under PAN's voice model

For a commercial PAN the biggest licence exposure is not a speech component but the voice model itself. OmniΠ is a fine-tune of LFM3-Omni-2B, and the LFM Open License v1.0 allows free commercial use only for organisations below **\$10M in annual revenue**, with a paid agreement above it; the same applies to LFM3-Audio if it is adopted ([Liquid licence](https://docs.liquid.ai/lfm/help/model-license)). That is workable today but should be a known cost line, with a permissively licensed fallback model qualified on the hub well before revenue approaches the threshold. The cleanest fallback is Gemma 4: Google moved the whole Gemma 4 family, including the E2B and E4B edge models, to Apache-2.0 in April 2026 (secondary reports; confirm on the model card) ([Implicator](https://www.implicator.ai/google-releases-gemma-4-under-apache-2-0-dropping-its-custom-ai-license/)). PAN has already measured gemma4:e2b reusing its cached prompt prefix in Ollama on the hub (internal), and the gemma4:31b teacher that labelled OmniΠ's training data is under the same licence, so rebasing OmniΠ onto Gemma 4 E2B during phase 2's answer-first retrain removes the revenue ceiling and the prefix-reuse problem together, at the cost of slower decoding (~22 versus ~35 tokens/s on the hub, internal). Qwen3.5's small models are also Apache-2.0 but interleave Gated DeltaNet linear-attention layers with attention three to one ([ApX](https://apxml.com/models/qwen35-2b); [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/alibabas-small-open-source-qwen3-5-9b-beats-openais-gpt-oss-120b-and-can-run)), so they share LFM3's prefix-reuse limitation. The second concentration is the phone voice: Supertonic's weights are under OpenRAIL-M, whose use-based restrictions need a careful read, and the project is archived, while several substitutes use GPL-3.0 code, since Piper moved to GPL-3.0 and the espeak-ng phonemiser used by Piper, Kokoro and Kitten voices in sherpa-onnx is reported as GPL-3.0 ([piper1-gpl](https://github.com/OHF-voice/piper1-gpl/releases); [PromptQuorum](https://www.promptquorum.com/power-local-llm/piper-tts-review)). PAN itself is licensed AGPL-3.0, which is compatible with GPL-3.0, so this matters only if the app is ever distributed as closed source ([LICENSE](../../../LICENSE)). Third, several of the best-measured turn-taking parts cannot be used outside their owners' products.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the question from which model PAN needs to which stages PAN lets overlap. PAN already owns most of the parts the leading systems use, an audio end-of-turn model, a streaming recogniser, an omni-capable fine-tune and a phone-side voice, and it loses time and naturalness in the joints between them: nothing happens while the user talks, nothing is heard while PAN talks, and nothing reaches the voice until the model has finished its bookkeeping. Several of PAN's conservative rules, flow mode's 1.1 s wait, the blind first 1.2 s of every reply and the discarding of anything said while PAN talks, are largely compensations for being half-duplex, so teaching PAN to listen while it speaks pays back latency as well as naturalness. On CPU the realistic median sits around 0.65-1.1 s, inside the commercial range but above the human window, which means the qualities that do not depend on raw speed, never cutting the user off, yielding within a quarter of a second, ignoring side talk and remembering, will decide whether PAN feels like a person.

Two strategic consequences follow. Building the talker/thinker split now gives PAN the same shape as GPT-Live-1 and Sesame's agent product, lifts its intelligence above the 2B ceiling without slowing easy turns, and lets a full-duplex talker or a larger thinker be swapped in later without rewriting the loop. And measurement has to be treated as part of the product rather than a precursor to it: the project's own regressions came from audio changes that no test could see, and the same harness that guards each phase is what will turn "it feels like a walkie-talkie" into numbers the owner can watch improve. The licence question deserves the same early attention, because the model at the centre of every phase is the one with a revenue ceiling.
